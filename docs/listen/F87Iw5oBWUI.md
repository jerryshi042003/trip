## Key points
- [0:00:30] The couple found Todaya, a Ningyocho shop that makes cloth for local businesses, festivals and kabuki actors, and learned that anyone can order their own design if the quantity is large enough. They made 500.
- [0:02:00] Fine detail, such as the food in six of their nine scenes, did not translate well to this art style and size. They swapped in bold, recognisable shapes (Mount Fuji, a torii, an onsen) and the design got cleaner.
- [0:03:00] The stencil (Ise katagami) was cut by a craftsperson near Suzuka in Mie. Carving takes five hours to two days, every piece stays joined by small bridges called tsuri, and the number of carvers has fallen sharply since the 1960s.
- [0:04:30] The cut stencil is lacquered onto a synthetic mesh; humidity matters so much that the workshop runs a humidifier all winter, and peeling off the paper is the hardest step.
- [0:06:30] Chusen dyeing at a factory in Edogawa-ku: resist paste is layered fold by fold, sawdust is added, and dye is pulled through the whole stack by a pedal-driven machine, which lets several colours share one stencil.
- [0:10:00] The dyers recommend 7 or 8 colours at most; more than 10 means constantly checking the order form and risking mistakes.
- [0:13:30] Third- and fourth-generation makers say they do new and pop designs, not only traditional ones, and want people to use tenugui casually, in a pocket or around the neck.
